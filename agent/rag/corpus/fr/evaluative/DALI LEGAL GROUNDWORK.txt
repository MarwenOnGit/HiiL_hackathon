DALI — LEGAL GROUNDWORK FOR ENGINEERING
PART I — CONTRACT FORMATION
“MAKE IT CLEAR”
0. THE CORE LEGAL LOGIC

Dali begins from a simple legal principle:

    A contract does not necessarily need to look like a formal contract to be legally meaningful.

Under the Tunisian Code of Obligations and Contracts (COC), the formation of an obligation depends principally on the required legal elements, not simply on whether the parties have produced a document called a “contract”.

For Dali, this creates four separate questions:
QUESTION 1 — WAS THERE AN AGREEMENT?

Did the parties exchange sufficiently clear expressions of will showing that they agreed?
QUESTION 2 — WHAT DID THEY AGREE TO?

What were the actual terms of the transaction?
QUESTION 3 — WHAT IS UNCLEAR OR MISSING?

Are any essential or important terms ambiguous, contradictory or absent?
QUESTION 4 — WHAT CAN BE PROVED?

What messages, documents, payments, signatures or other evidence support each part of the transaction?

Dali must not collapse these four questions into one.

A transaction may have a potentially valid agreement but poor evidence.

A transaction may have abundant evidence but unclear contractual terms.

A transaction may have clear terms but a problem concerning the authority of the person who acted for a company.

This distinction is fundamental to the architecture of the software.
1. LEGAL BASIS: THE FOUR ELEMENTS OF THE OBLIGATION
Main source

Code des Obligations et des Contrats, Article 2, together with the provisions governing consent, object, cause and contractual obligations.

For the MVP, Dali should work with four basic legal elements:
Legal element	Simple meaning	What Dali checks
Capacity	Can the person legally undertake the obligation?	Identity, role and apparent authority
Consent	Did the parties actually agree?	Offer, acceptance and subsequent changes
Object	What is being promised?	Goods, services, quantity, specifications
Cause	Why is the obligation undertaken?	Economic/legal purpose of the transaction

These are legal checkpoints, not four boxes that AI can mechanically “approve”.

The AI should identify information relevant to each checkpoint and flag uncertainty.

It should not automatically declare:

    “Contract valid.”

Instead, it should say things such as:

    “The available exchanges show an apparent agreement concerning the sale of 500 units.”

or:

    “The parties appear to have agreed on the price and quantity, but the delivery date remains unclear.”

2. CONSENT — DID THE PARTIES AGREE?
2.1 What Dali needs to detect

Dali should identify communications showing:
A. An offer or proposal

Example:

    “I can supply 500 chairs at 12 TND each, delivery before 15 September.”

B. Acceptance

Example:

    “OK, agreed.”

C. Counter-offer

Example:

    “I agree, but only at 11 TND.”

This is not necessarily acceptance of the original proposal. It may represent a new proposal.
D. Conditional acceptance

Example:

    “Agreed, provided you deliver before 15 September.”

The condition is part of the contractual analysis.
E. Rejection

Example:

    “No, that price doesn't work.”

F. Later modification

Example:

    “Actually, make it 600 units instead of 500.”

Dali must treat later communications as potentially modifying the original transaction.
3. CONSENT — THE SYSTEM MUST FOLLOW THE CONVERSATION, NOT JUST EXTRACT SENTENCES

This is one of the most important engineering principles.

The AI should not analyse each message independently.

It should reconstruct the sequence of negotiations.
Example

Message 1 — Seller

    “500 pieces at 12 TND each, delivery by 15 September.”

Message 2 — Buyer

    “Can you do 10 TND?”

Message 3 — Seller

    “11 TND.”

Message 4 — Buyer

    “OK.”

The system should produce:

Final apparent agreement

    Quantity: 500

    Unit price: 11 TND

    Delivery deadline: 15 September

    Acceptance: buyer's “OK”

    Earlier price of 12 TND: superseded by later exchange

It should not extract both 12 TND and 11 TND as if they were simultaneously operative terms.
4. CONSENT — SILENCE IS NOT AUTOMATIC ACCEPTANCE

Dali should not treat:

    “I sent you the quotation.”

followed by no response

as equivalent to:

    “I accept.”

The system should distinguish:

    proposal sent;

    proposal viewed/received, if evidence exists;

    discussion continued;

    explicit acceptance;

    conduct potentially indicating acceptance;

    no evidence of acceptance.

System rule

Do not infer acceptance merely from silence unless the legal context clearly supports such an inference.

If uncertain:

    “No clear acceptance was identified in the available exchanges.”

5. OFFER + DEADLINE

Where the available legal rules make an offer binding for a stated period, Dali should capture:

    date of offer;

    content of offer;

    deadline for acceptance;

    whether the deadline has expired;

    whether the offer was withdrawn;

    whether acceptance occurred before expiry.

Data structure

OFFER
├── offeror
├── proposed terms
├── date/time
├── acceptance deadline
├── acceptance identified?
└── status

Important limitation

The software should distinguish:

“An offer exists”

from

“A binding contract resulted from that offer.”

The second requires analysis of the subsequent response and the other formation elements.
6. OBJECT — WHAT IS THE TRANSACTION ABOUT?

The object is the substance of what the parties undertake to provide, do, deliver, pay or perform.

Dali should convert informal descriptions into structured information.
Example

User's message:

    “I need 500 cotton T-shirts, black, sizes M and L, delivered before the 15th.”

Dali should extract:

OBJECT
Type: Goods
Product: T-shirts
Material: Cotton
Colour: Black
Quantity: 500
Sizes: M / L
Delivery deadline: 15 September

7. OBJECT — “WHAT” IS MORE IMPORTANT THAN PERFECT FORMAL WORDING

The system should not require a sophisticated contractual description.

The question is:

    Can the subject of the obligation be identified or determined?

The user's legal groundwork relies on COC Article 63, under which the object may be determined at least as to its kind.

Therefore, Dali should distinguish:
CLEAR

    “500 office chairs, model X.”

DETERMINABLE

    “All chairs from the model shown in the attached quotation.”

INSUFFICIENTLY CLEAR

    “Send me the usual chairs.”

CONTRADICTORY

Message 1:

    “500 units.”

Later message:

    “Actually, we agreed on 300.”

The last situation should trigger a conflict flag, not an automatic choice.
8. QUANTITY DOES NOT ALWAYS HAVE TO BE FIXED AT THE FIRST EXCHANGE

Dali should therefore avoid the rule:

    “No exact quantity = no contract.”

Instead, distinguish:
Quantity fixed

    500 units.

Quantity determinable

    “The quantity shown on the confirmed purchase order.”

Quantity still unresolved

    “Send me what you have available.”

The third situation requires clarification.
Suggested user prompt

    “The product is identified, but the quantity is not yet clear. Please confirm the quantity.”

9. PRICE AND PAYMENT

Although price is not one of the four labels in Article 2, it is a critical transaction term for Dali because it defines the parties' economic obligations.

Capture:

    total price;

    unit price;

    currency;

    taxes, if mentioned;

    deposit/advance;

    balance;

    payment deadline;

    payment method;

    payment conditions;

    payment trigger.

Example

    “6,000 TND total. 50% now, 50% after delivery.”

Dali creates:

TOTAL = 6,000 TND

PAYMENT 1
Amount = 3,000 TND
Timing = immediately / before delivery

PAYMENT 2
Amount = 3,000 TND
Trigger = delivery

This becomes extremely important later for the dispute module.
10. DEADLINES AND CONDITIONS

Dali must distinguish different types of time-related information.
A. Fixed date

    “Delivery on 15 September.”

B. Period

    “Within 10 days.”

C. Event-based deadline

    “Payment after delivery.”

D. Approximate timing

    “Around next week.”

E. Unclear timing

    “Deliver soon.”

These should not be treated equally.
Suggested classification

🟢 Precise

🟠 Determinable but needs confirmation

🔴 Ambiguous
11. CONDITIONS

The AI should detect terms beginning with or equivalent to:

    if;

    provided that;

    unless;

    subject to;

    only if;

    once;

    after;

    before.

Example:

    “I'll pay the balance once the goods are delivered.”

Dali should establish a relationship:

DELIVERY
      ↓
TRIGGERS
      ↓
BALANCE PAYMENT

This is important because it later becomes part of the smart-contract logic.
12. AMBIGUITY — THE MOST IMPORTANT “MAKE IT CLEAR” FEATURE

Dali should not attempt to make an ambiguous agreement look precise.

Its role is to detect ambiguity and bring it back to the parties.
Example

    “If you're late, we'll reduce the price.”

The system should not invent:

    “Price reduced by 10%.”

Instead:

    “The agreement refers to a price reduction in case of delay, but the amount or method of calculation is not specified.”

Then:

    “Would you like to clarify the consequence of late delivery?”

Possible structured choices:

    Fixed amount

    Percentage

    Amount per day of delay

    Other

13. CONTRADICTIONS

Dali must detect when different pieces of evidence contain different terms.
Example

Quotation:

    500 units.

WhatsApp:

    “Let's make it 600.”

Invoice:

    600 units.

Dali should display:
⚠️ Quantity changed
Source	Quantity
Quotation	500
WhatsApp	600
Invoice	600

Then ask:

    “The quantity appears to have changed from 500 to 600. Which quantity reflects the final agreement?”

This is much safer than choosing the latest document automatically.
14. MODIFICATIONS TO THE AGREEMENT

A transaction should be treated as dynamic.

After initial agreement, parties may modify:

    quantity;

    price;

    delivery date;

    payment schedule;

    specifications;

    responsibilities;

    penalties/consequences.

Therefore, Dali needs:

ORIGINAL AGREEMENT
        ↓
MODIFICATION 1
        ↓
MODIFICATION 2
        ↓
CURRENT AGREED TERMS

Each modification should preserve:

    source;

    date/time;

    parties involved;

    old term;

    new term;

    evidence.

This creates a contract history rather than a static document.
15. CAPACITY AND AUTHORITY

This requires a particularly careful distinction.
Capacity

Basic question:

    Is this person legally capable of undertaking the obligation?

Authority

Different question:

    Is this person acting for the business, and do they appear authorized to bind it?

Dali should not confuse the two.
Example

Ahmed communicates:

    “I confirm the order on behalf of ABC SARL.”

Dali should record:

Person communicating: Ahmed
Claimed role: Representative of ABC SARL
Contracting party: ABC SARL
Authority evidence: Not provided

The correct output is not:

    “Ahmed cannot bind ABC SARL.”

The correct output is:

    “Ahmed appears to be acting on behalf of ABC SARL. No information confirming his authority was identified in the material provided.”

This is a risk flag, not a legal conclusion.
16. PARTY IDENTIFICATION

Before Dali can analyse the agreement, it should build a Party Record.

For each party:

PARTY
├── Name
├── Business name
├── Individual / legal entity
├── Role
├── Contact information
├── Registration information
├── Tax identification, if provided
├── Representative, if applicable
├── Authority evidence, if available
└── Verification status

17. BUSINESS REGISTRATION

The RNE framework is relevant to Dali's party-identification layer. The 2018 legislation provides for registration of, among others, natural persons having merchant status and persons carrying on artisanal or professional activities.

But the product should not turn registration into a condition for using Dali.

The distinction should be:

    “Not verified” ≠ “legally invalid.”

Dali can record:
VERIFIED

Registration information provided or verified.
PARTIALLY VERIFIED

Some business information exists but verification is incomplete.
NOT VERIFIED

No registration information has been provided.
CONFLICT

Information provided by the parties does not match.
18. WHY PARTY VERIFICATION MATTERS

The purpose is not to decide whether a contract exists.

The purpose is to improve:

    identification;

    evidence;

    accountability;

    later dispute resolution;

    communication;

    enforcement preparation.

Example:

    “Ahmed” may be enough to communicate informally.

But for a dispute, the user may need to establish:

    Who exactly was the contracting party?

Dali therefore turns informal identity information into a structured record.
19. CAUSE — KEEP THIS SIMPLE IN THE MVP

The legal concept of cause should be included in the legal model but should not become a complicated AI decision tree.

For ordinary commercial transactions, Dali can capture the apparent economic purpose:

    sale of goods → payment of price

    provision of services → payment of fees

    supply arrangement → reciprocal performance

The AI should primarily flag situations where the apparent purpose is:

    missing;

    contradictory;

    apparently unlawful;

    inconsistent with the transaction described.

It should not attempt to determine complex questions of legality autonomously.
Safe output

    “The stated purpose of this transaction is unclear. Please provide more information.”

Not:

    “This contract is legally invalid.”

20. DALI'S INTERNAL CONTRACT STATUS

The engineering team should not use a single field called:

contract_valid = true/false

That is too crude and legally dangerous.

Instead use separate statuses.
Suggested model

FORMATION_STATUS
├── No apparent agreement
├── Agreement developing
├── Apparent agreement
├── Agreement with unresolved terms
└── Requires human/legal review

And separately:

EVIDENCE_STATUS
├── Weak
├── Partial
├── Good
└── Strong

And:

IDENTITY_STATUS
├── Unverified
├── Partially verified
└── Verified

And:

TERM_STATUS
├── Clear
├── Determinable
├── Ambiguous
└── Contradictory

This separation is one of the most important architectural decisions in the whole product.

22. EVIDENCE MUST BE LINKED TO TERMS

This is essential for the next stage of Dali.

The system should not merely store documents in a folder.

Each important contractual term should be linked to its source.
Example

Term: Quantity = 500 units

Evidence:

    WhatsApp message — 10 September, 14:32

    Quotation — PDF, page 1

    Purchase order — PDF, page 1

Therefore:

TERM
Quantity = 500

SUPPORTED BY
Evidence 01
Evidence 04
Evidence 07

Later, if the seller delivers 350 units, Dali can connect:

CONTRACTUAL OBLIGATION
500 units

VS.

PERFORMANCE EVIDENCE
350 units delivered

↓

POTENTIAL NON-PERFORMANCE

That is the bridge between Make it Clear and Make it Provable.
23. WHAT DALI SHOULD SAY TO THE USER

The interface should use simple language, even though the underlying legal model is sophisticated.

Instead of:

    “The essential conditions of validity under Article 2 appear to be satisfied.”

Use:

    “We found an apparent agreement.”

Then:

    “Here is what we understood.”

Then:

    “Two points still need clarification.”

This gives the user legal structure without pretending that the AI is issuing a legal judgment.
24. THE USER CONFIRMATION STEP

After extraction, Dali should ask the parties to confirm the structured record.
Screen:
“Here is what we understood”

Buyer: ABC Café
Seller: Ahmed / XYZ Supplies
Goods: 500 chairs
Price: 12 TND/unit
Total: 6,000 TND
Deposit: 3,000 TND
Balance: 3,000 TND after delivery
Delivery: 15 September
Then:

    “Is this correct?”

Buttons:

Confirm

Edit

Something is missing

This confirmation is crucial because AI extraction should remain distinguishable from the parties' own agreement.
25. WHAT THE AI MUST NEVER DO

For this module, the following are hard constraints.
DO NOT:

    invent contractual terms;

    assume acceptance from silence;

    automatically choose between contradictory statements;

    declare a contract invalid merely because it is informal;

    declare a contract valid merely because four pieces of information were extracted;

    assume that a person represents a company without qualification;

    treat missing registration as automatic invalidity;

    silently interpret ambiguous penalties or conditions;

    convert an uncertain legal conclusion into a fact;

    present an AI-generated interpretation as if it were the parties' agreement.

DO:

    identify;

    structure;

    compare;

    flag;

    ask;

    preserve the source;

    let the parties confirm.

26. ENGINEERING LOGIC — SIMPLE VERSION

The engineers can reduce the legal model to this workflow:

INPUT
Messages + voice notes + quotes + invoices + payment/delivery evidence
                    ↓

                    ↓

                    ↓
3. IDENTIFY ACCEPTANCE / REJECTION / COUNTER-OFFERS
                    ↓

                    ↓

                    ↓

                    ↓

                    ↓

                    ↓
12. LOCK CONFIRMED VERSION

27. THE LEGAL PRINCIPLE BEHIND THE FEATURE

The product's legal proposition can therefore be stated very simply:

    Dali does not create a contract merely by putting informal communications into a template. It identifies and structures the agreement that may already exist, makes uncertainty visible, and creates a clearer record of what the parties actually agreed.

That distinction is central to the product's legal credibility.
MODULE 2 — PARTY IDENTITY AND COMMERCIAL STATUS
1. PURPOSE

The purpose of this module is not to decide whether a person is legally entitled to contract.

Its purpose is to answer:

    Who are the parties, in what capacity are they acting, and what information supports their identity?

2. PARTY TYPES

Dali should distinguish:
A. Individual acting personally

    “I am buying 100 units for my shop.”

B. Individual trader / professional

Person appears to conduct business personally.
C. Legal entity

Company or other legal entity is identified as the contracting party.
D. Representative

A natural person communicates or signs on behalf of another person/entity.
3. REPRESENTATION

If:

    “Ahmed, manager of XYZ SARL, confirms the order.”

Dali should store:

CONTRACTING PARTY = XYZ SARL

ACTING PERSON = Ahmed

CLAIMED ROLE = Manager

AUTHORITY EVIDENCE = Not yet provided

If a company document or other evidence identifies Ahmed's role:

AUTHORITY EVIDENCE = Provided

Again:

absence of evidence of authority ≠ automatic conclusion that there is no authority.

It is a verification issue.
4. REGISTRATION INFORMATION

Where available, Dali can collect:

    legal/business name;

    RNE identifier;

    tax identifier;

    business address;

    business activity;

    registration documents.

But the system should treat these as verification information, not as the sole determinant of contractual existence.

The RNE framework makes registration an important part of identifying economic operators.
5. IDENTITY CONFLICTS

Dali should detect inconsistencies.

Example:

WhatsApp:

    “This is Ahmed from ABC SARL.”

Quotation:

    “ABC Trading SARL.”

Invoice:

    “ABC Commerce.”

Dali should flag:

    “The business names in your documents do not appear identical. Please confirm which entity is the contracting party.”

This is much more useful than simply saying:

    “Identity mismatch.”

6. PARTY CONFIRMATION

Before finalizing the transaction record:

    “Please confirm the parties to this transaction.”

Then display:

XYZ SARL

The first two modules should ultimately feed one unified structure:

   - representative
   - authority
   - verification

B. FORMATION
   - offer
   - acceptance
   - counter-offer
   - modifications
   - consent status

C. TERMS
   - object
   - quantity
   - specifications
   - price
   - payment
   - deadlines
   - delivery
   - conditions

D. EVIDENCE
   - source
   - date/time
   - document/message
   - linked term

E. UNCERTAINTY
   - missing
   - ambiguous
   - contradictory
   - unverified

F. USER CONFIRMATION
   - confirmed terms
   - corrections
   - confirmation date
   - confirmation by party

The guiding rule
Extract → Structure → Compare → Flag → Ask → Confirm → Preserve evidence

That should be the legal-engineering philosophy of Dali's “Make it Clear” module.

Only after this record is confirmed should the system move into the next layer:

MAKE IT PROVABLE → monitor performance against the confirmed obligations → identify potential non-performance → build the evidence timeline → prepare for early resolution.
